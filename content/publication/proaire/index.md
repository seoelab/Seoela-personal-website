abstract: Air quality regulations often target pollution where exposure is
  highest, creating spatial differences in both compliance costs and
  environmental benefits. This paper studies one such intervention, Mexico's
  ProAire air-quality programs, and their effects on manufacturing emissions,
  firm trade, and consumer welfare. I combine the staggered rollout of ProAire
  across municipalities from 2011 to 2020 with linked firm-level data. I first
  show that the decline in Mexican manufacturing emissions intensity between
  2005 and 2018 was driven mainly by within-industry technique changes, which
  account for 88 percent of the reduction. After ProAire adoption, reported CO2
  emissions among RETC-reporting firms fall by 11 percent. Trade adjustment
  occurs primarily through the extensive and intensive margins of exporting.
  Export entry changes little, export exit rises by 2.8 percentage points, and
  export sales among continuing exporters fall by 9.5 percent, while imports
  among continuing importers rise by 18 percent. I then evaluate the welfare
  implications of spatially targeted regulation through a quantitative spatial
  model with heterogeneous firms, pollution-intensive production, international
  trade, atmospheric transport, and worker sorting. I find that ProAire lowers
  output by 0.07 percent, while welfare rises by 0.099 percent.
